Hindu Temples of Pakistan

Discover Pakistan's rich Hindu heritage, from sacred pond complexes and mountain temples to living shrines where centuries-old devotional traditions continue alongside modern life.

Sacred Geography

Long before the partition of 1947, the land now called Pakistan was home to vibrant Hindu communities, sacred pilgrimage sites, and temples spanning from ancient times to the medieval period. While many temples fell into disuse or disrepair, recent years have seen significant restoration efforts, government protection, and the revival of pilgrimage—including visitors from India and the Pakistani Hindu community celebrating their heritage. To visit these sites is to witness a layer of Pakistan's identity that challenges simplistic narratives and reveals deep cultural continuities.

Major Temple Sites

  • Katas Raj Temple Complex (Punjab) — Pakistan's most famous Hindu pilgrimage site, located near Chakwal in the Salt Range. The complex centers on a sacred pond (katas, meaning "raining eyes") said to have been formed by Shiva's tears. Seven ancient temples surround the pond, with origins dating back to the 6th-7th centuries CE, though the site may have been sacred even earlier. The temples feature Kashmiri-style architecture and intricate carvings. The complex also includes a British-era haveli and a nearby Mughal-era caravanserai. The pond itself is fed by natural springs and is believed by devotees to have healing properties.
  • Hinglaj Mata Temple (Balochistan) — One of the 51 Shakti Peethas (sacred sites where parts of goddess Sati's body fell), Hinglaj is considered the most important Hindu pilgrimage site in Pakistan. Located in the barren Hingol National Park near the Makran coast, the temple is built into a natural cave. Each year, thousands of Pakistani Hindus and visitors from India undertake the arduous pilgrimage during the April Hinglaj Yatra. The surrounding landscape—mud volcanoes, dramatic rock formations resembling sphinxes—adds mystical atmosphere. The pilgrimage is also respected by local Muslim communities, who sometimes participate in supporting the yatra.
  • Varun Dev Mandir, Manora Island (Karachi) — An active temple dedicated to the Vedic god of the oceans, located on Manora Island at the entrance to Karachi harbor. The original temple dates to ancient times, with the current structure built in the 19th century. The temple remains a living place of worship for Karachi's Hindu community, particularly fishermen who seek blessings before voyages. The annual Jhule Lal festival celebrates the Sindhi patron saint here.
  • Shri Swaminarayan Mandir, Karachi — One of the largest and most ornate Hindu temples in Pakistan, completed in 2004. Built in traditional Gujarati architectural style with intricate marble carvings, the temple serves the city's vibrant Hindu community and welcomes visitors of all faiths. It's a testament to modern religious pluralism in Pakistan.
  • Prahladpuri Temple (Multan) — An ancient temple dedicated to Prahlada, the devotee prince from Hindu mythology. The site has been sacred for over 2,000 years, though the current structure dates to various periods. Located within Multan Fort, it coexists with Sufi shrines—a symbol of the region's layered religious history.
  • Sadhu Bela, Sukkur (Sindh) — A temple complex built on an island in the Indus River, established in 1889 by a Hindu ascetic. The island features gardens, temples, and rest houses. Despite its relatively recent origins, it has become an important pilgrimage site and is beautifully maintained. The complex showcases late-19th-century Hindu temple architecture and offers serene river views.
  • Shiv Mandir, Umerkot (Sindh) — Umerkot, birthplace of Mughal emperor Akbar, has a significant Hindu population and several active temples. The main Shiv temple is a center of community life and hosts vibrant festivals, particularly during Maha Shivaratri. Umerkot's Hindu temples are remarkable for being continuously used places of worship.

Heritage & Context

Ancient Roots

The Indus Valley Civilization (3300-1300 BCE) shows evidence of ritual practices that may have influenced later Hinduism. Hindu kingdoms, including the Rai Dynasty of Sindh, ruled parts of Pakistan until the 8th century CE. Many temples date from this pre-Islamic period, while others were built under Hindu administrators during later Muslim-ruled eras.

Architectural Styles

Pakistan's Hindu temples showcase diverse styles—Kashmiri (Katas Raj), Sindh regional styles (Sadhu Bela), Gujarati (Swaminarayan), and Rajasthani influences. Many feature distinctive shikhara towers, ornate carvings, sacred water tanks, and mandapa halls. Some incorporate Mughal-era architectural influences.

Living Communities

Pakistan's Hindu population (approximately 2-4 million, mostly in Sindh) maintains vibrant religious practices. Festivals like Holi, Diwali, Maha Shivaratri, and Janmashtami are celebrated openly. Temples serve as community centers, schools, and cultural hubs. Visiting active temples offers insight into contemporary Hindu life in Pakistan.

Restoration Efforts

Recent years have seen increased government and private efforts to restore and protect Hindu heritage sites. The Pakistan Hindu Council, Evacuee Trust Property Board, and cultural organizations work to preserve temples. Diplomatic initiatives allow Indian pilgrims to visit sacred sites like Katas Raj and Hinglaj through special visa programs.

Pilgrimage & Festivals

Katas Raj Mela: Held during the Hindu months of Phalguna and Kartik, this festival attracts thousands of pilgrims who circumambulate the sacred pond, bathe in its waters, and worship at the surrounding temples.

Hinglaj Yatra: The annual April pilgrimage to Hinglaj Mata in Balochistan is Pakistan's largest Hindu religious gathering. Pilgrims travel hundreds of miles through the desert, camping along the way. The yatra includes rituals at the temple, mud volcano (chandragup), and various sacred spots. The Pakistani government provides security and support, and local Muslim communities offer hospitality.

Sindh Festivals: Sindh's Hindu-majority areas celebrate Holi with color throwing, music, and communal feasts. Diwali lights up homes and temples. Janmashtami (Krishna's birthday) sees processions and devotional singing. These festivals are increasingly recognized as part of Pakistan's cultural heritage.

What to Expect

Access: Most temples welcome respectful visitors of all backgrounds. Remove shoes before entering temple premises, dress modestly, and ask permission before photographing. Active temples may have specific visiting hours around prayer times (arti).

Cultural Sensitivity: Pakistan's Hindu communities have faced challenges, and their heritage sites deserve respectful engagement. We work with local community leaders to ensure visits benefit temples through donations and awareness while respecting privacy and religious practices.

Logistics: Some sites (like Hinglaj) require special permits and advance planning. Others (like Katas Raj) are easily accessible. We arrange all necessary permissions, cultural guides who can explain iconography and traditions, and connections with local communities when appropriate.

Beyond Temples

Hindu heritage tourism in Pakistan intersects with other rich layers—many temples are near Sufi shrines (the syncretic culture of Sindh), ancient trade routes, Mughal monuments, and natural wonders. Sindh's Thar Desert, home to many temples, offers camel safaris, traditional villages, and distinct desert culture. Balochistan's Hingol National Park (site of Hinglaj) features otherworldly landscapes and diverse wildlife.

A Story of Coexistence

Pakistan's Hindu heritage challenges outsider stereotypes and reveals stories of coexistence, shared devotion (many Sufi shrines are visited by Hindus, and some Hindu sites by Muslims), and cultural continuity despite political divisions. Recent restoration efforts and government-supported pilgrimages signal growing recognition of this heritage as integral to Pakistan's identity. To visit these sites is to witness resilience, faith, and the possibility of pluralism.
